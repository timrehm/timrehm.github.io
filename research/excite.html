Science Introduction

Phase-resolved spectroscopy is the method of continuously observing transiting exoplanets throughout their entire oribts and measuring the spectral composition of the light from both the star and exoplanet. A transiting exoplanet is one that moves in front of its star along our line of sight. Think of a solar eclipse; the Moon passes in front of the Sun from our viewpoint, physically blocks sunlight from reaching Earth, and causes a momentary period of darkness. The same thing happens when we measure transiting exoplanet. A dip in brightness occurs, called the "transit depth," when the exoplanet passes in front of the star since it physically blocks light from reaching Earth. Not all exoplanets transit from our line of sight. A similar effect occurs when the exoplanet reaches the opposite side of its orbit, this time moving behind the star. At this point, we only measure flux from the star since the exoplanet is obscured. Because the exoplanet itself emits flux, either from reflected starlight or thermal emission, this obscuration results in a second brightness dip, called the "secondary eclispe depth" or just the "eclipse depth." Whereas the transit depth (\( \delta_t \)) results in ~1% dip in the total brightess of the system, the eclipse depth (\( D_e \)) is almost always much smaller, on the order of ~0.001—0.0001% of the total brightness. To first order, you can calculate \( \delta_t = R^2_\star / R^2_p \) and \( D_e = \delta_t[BB(T_p, \lambda)/BB(T_\star, \lambda)] \), where \( R_p \ \& \ T_p \) are the radius and temperature of the exoplanet, \( R_\star \ \& \ T_\star \) are the radius and temperature of the star, and \( BB \) refers to the blackbody flux, or Planck function. If one continuously measures a transiting exoplanet throughout its entire orbit, connecting the transit and the eclipse, you end up with what is called a "phase curve." Think again of the Moon when referring to the term "phase." When an exoplanet is close to transit, only a small portion of the bright dayside will face Earth, and this is akin to a waxing or waning crescent. When an exoplanet is close to eclipse, a large portion of the bright dayside will face Earth, similar to a waxing or waning gibbous. The biggest difference is that we do not see a full Moon equivalent, because the exoplanet is hidden when the dayside is fully pointed towards Earth. Phase curves give information on the physical paramters of an exoplanet: radius, mass, and effective temperature. Recently, a large effort has been put forth to measure the wavelength dependence of phase curves, called spectroscopic phase curves, in order to constrain the properties of an exoplanet's atmosphere. Depending on the size and chemical composition of an exoplanet's atmosphere, the transit, eclispe depth, and flux at intermediate phases will vary as a function of wavelength. Therefore, by observing phase curves across a range of wavelengths, i.e., phase-resolved spectroscopy, we can create longitudinal maps at different altitudes, as different wavelengths probe different pressures, providing measurements of the atmospheric composition and vertical temperature structure as a function of longitude. Probing the chemical inventory and thermal state of an exoplanet's atmosphere provides key insights into its formation and evolution. However, these measurements are resource-intensive for general-purpose observatories, such as the James Webb Space Telescope (JWST), and are dominated by tellurics and systematics on the ground and in low-Earth orbit, such as the Hubble Space Telescope (HST). As a result, only a few full-orbit phase-resolved spectra have been published. That's where EXCITE comes in.

Balloon Observing

EXCITE is the first mission dedicated to phase-resolved spectroscopy and will do so from a high-altitude balloon platform at 25 miles (~40 km) above the ground, above 99.5% of Earth's atmosphere. EXCITE carries a 0.5-meter diameter telescope and measures the spectral composition of light from exoplant-star systems across 0.8—3.5 μm. Scientific balloons are made from polyethylene film that is as thin as a Ziploc plastic bag and are filled with a lot of Helium. These aren't normal party balloons. At float altitudes, the balloon EXCITE uses is filled with 39 million cubic feet of Helium. That's about the size of a football stadium! Flying from a balloon gets you near-space observing conditions which are transparent and stable in the near-infrared. EXCITE is designed to fly from Earth's poles and will launch from the NASA Columbia Scientific Balloon Facility (CSBF) at McMurdo Station, Antarctica during the Winter 2026-27. Balloon payloads launched during the Austral Summer from Antarctica benefit from circumpolar winds which carry them in large circles around the continent. This allows for long-duration balloon (LDB) flights that last on average of 15—20 days, with the maximum being 58 days. The right hand side shows the flight path of the BLAST-Pol mission during a 2010 LDB flight. It completed one revolution around the continent in about 10 days.

An LDB flight platform is great for phase-resolved spectroscopy. Since EXCITE can fly for multiple weeks from the poles, targets will never set, and the telescope can stare continuously on one exoplanet-star system for the exoplanet's entire orbital period. EXCITE will observe a type of exoplanet called "hot Jupiters,'' which, as you could surmise, are as large as Jupiter and very hot, having dayside temperatures between 1000—4000 Kelvin (1340—6740\(^\circ\)F). But unlike our Jupiter, hot Jupiters orbit their host stars much closer, at about a distance of 0.1 Astronomical Units, almost four-times closer than the distance Mercury is from the Sun! We observe these exoplanets because they have large, extended atmospheres and have orbital periods between 1—5 days, which make for great candidates for atmospheric studies and make continuous, full-orbit observations feasible. If EXCITE flies for 18 days from Antarctica, it could measure phase-resolved spectra of up to 6 different targets! That would essentially match the amount that currently exists.

Mission Design

EXCITE broken down into two main components: a balloon gondola and a science instrument. The balloon gondola is the housing structure that contains the science instrument, attaches to the balloon, and is responsible for high-stability pointing of the telescope. The science instrument comprises the telescope and a cryogenic receiver, the latter of which contains a cryogenically cooled spectrograph and a detector. A spectrograph is a device that spilts the light focused by the telescope into different wavelengths and images the dispersed light onto a focal plane. The right photo shows the complete payload. In the top-left photo, the telescope is on the left, and the cryogenic receiver is the blue dewar on the right. There are two smaller star cameras attached to the telescope tube to aid in pointing and determining absolute orientation on the sky.

Common questions people ask about balloon payloads are, "How are you able to stabilize it? Isn't the balloon rocking and moving it around? Wouldn't it get all twisted up?" These are great questions because the balloon and the high-strength suspension cables which connect it to the payload, called the "flight train," move and twist and pendulate the payload, and we need to account for that. Since we measure very small flux vairations from exoplanet-star systems over the course of days, our telescope requires very high-stability pointing. To do this, our gondola has three-axes motion and uses a suite of motors, encoders, gyroscopes, aforementioned star cameras, and robust computing algorithms to stabilize the boresight of the telescope down to 50 milliarcseconds at the detector. This is the equivalent of a holding a gaze on a U.S. quarter coin from 65 miles away!
