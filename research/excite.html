The EXoplanet Climate Infrared TElescope (EXCITE) is a NASA-funded mission that flies on a scientific balloon. It characterizes the atmospheres of exoplanets via a technique called phase-resolved spectroscopy. Here's a photo of me with the EXCITE telescope:

Phase-resolved spectroscopy is the method of continuously observing transiting exoplanets throughout their entire oribts around their host star and measuring the spectral composition of the light from both the star and exoplanet. A transiting exoplanet is one that moves in front of its star along our line of sight. Think of a solar eclipse; the Moon passes in front of the Sun from our viewpoint, physically blocks sunlight from reaching Earth, and causes a momentary period of darkness. The same thing happens when we measure transiting exoplanet. A dip in brightness occurs, called the "transit depth," when the exoplanet passes in front of the star since it physically blocks light from reaching Earth. A similar effect occurs when the exoplanet reaches the opposite side of its orbit, this time moving behind the star. At this point, we only measure flux from the star since the exoplanet is obscured. Because the exoplanet itself emits flux, either from reflected starlight or thermal emission, this obscuration results in a second brightness dip, called the "secondary eclispe depth" or just the "eclipse depth." Whereas the transit depth, \( \delta_2 \), results in ~1% dip in the total brightess of the system, the eclipse depth, \( D_e \), is almost always much smaller, on the order of ~0.001-0.0001% the total brightness. EXCITE carries a 0.5-meter diameter telescope and measures the spectral composition of light from exoplant-star systems across 0.8—3.5 μm. Flying from a balloon gets you near-space observing conditions which are transparent and stable in the near-infrared.
